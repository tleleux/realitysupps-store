A certificate of analysis (COA) is the document a testing laboratory issues for one production lot of a compound. For a research peptide it is the only objective evidence a buyer has about what is in the vial. Reading one takes two minutes once you know where to look.

## What a certificate must contain

A usable certificate names five things. If any are missing, treat the document as marketing, not analysis.

| Field | What it tells you | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Laboratory | Who ran the test | A named, independent lab with a street address. In-house "QC" is not independent. |
| Lot or batch number | Which production run was tested | It must match the number printed on the vial label, character for character. |
| Test date | How recent the analysis is | Within the last few months for an active lot. A certificate from a lot that sold out a year ago says nothing about the vial in hand. |
| Method | How purity and identity were measured | HPLC for purity, mass spectrometry for identity. One without the other is half an answer. |
| Result | The measured figure | A number, such as 98.6 percent, not a range and not a threshold like "≥98 percent" copied from a label. |

## Purity and identity are different tests

Purity, measured by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), is the share of the sample that elutes as the main peak. A figure of 98 percent means 2 percent of the material is something else: truncated sequences, deletion products or residual reagents from synthesis.

Identity, measured by mass spectrometry (MS), confirms that the main peak has the molecular weight of the compound named on the label. A sample can be 99 percent pure and still be the wrong molecule. A certificate that reports purity without a mass result has not confirmed identity.

Some laboratories add a third panel: net content (how many milligrams are actually in the vial), endotoxin and sterility. These are more expensive and less common. Their absence is not a red flag; their presence is a good sign.

## Reading the chromatogram

Most certificates include the HPLC trace. Three features matter:

1. **One dominant peak.** The target compound should be a single tall peak. Several peaks of similar height indicate a mixture.
2. **Retention time.** The time at which the main peak elutes should be consistent across lots of the same compound from the same lab. A shifted retention time with the same purity figure deserves a question.
3. **Baseline.** A noisy or rising baseline can hide small impurity peaks. Clean certificates show a flat baseline on either side of the main peak.

## Four checks before trusting a certificate

1. The lot on the certificate is the lot on the vial.
2. The laboratory is named and can be looked up.
3. The method is stated and includes both HPLC and MS.
4. The certificate is published as issued, with the lab's own formatting, not retyped into the supplier's template.

The fourth check matters more than it looks. Altered or fabricated certificates were a central fact in the only research-peptide case to date that ended in a prison sentence (United States v. Kawa, N.D. Indiana, sentenced July 2026). A supplier that publishes certificates unedited is taking on accountability for them.
